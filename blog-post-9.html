The First Six Weeks: How to Set Up a Strong School Year

September 15, 2026 TutorWise

The first couple of weeks of school are now behind us. Backpacks are packed, schedules are settling and the excitement of September is starting to fade into regular routines. This is actually one of the most important times of the year, because the habits your child builds now tend to stick for the next ten months.

Here is how to make the most of the first six weeks.

Why the First Weeks Matter So Much

Teachers use September and early October to review last year's material, introduce new expectations and get to know their students. They are also forming their first impressions of each child's work habits.

In Ontario, those habits are reported on directly. Every report card includes learning skills like responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative and self-regulation. Building good routines early shows up on the very first progress report.

Build a Homework Routine That Actually Sticks

The best homework routine is one your family can keep. A few things help:

Same time, same place. Whether it is right after school or after dinner, consistency makes it easier for kids to get started without a battle.

Start with a plan. Before diving in, have your child list what needs to be done and what is due soonest.

Keep it reasonable. If homework regularly takes much longer than the teacher expects, that is useful information to share with the school.

Even on nights with no homework, a short reading session keeps the routine alive.

Learn How Your Child's Teacher Communicates

Most schools now use online platforms like Google Classroom or Brightspace to post assignments, announcements and marks. Ask your child to show you where their work lives, and check in once a week.

Find out how each teacher prefers to be contacted. A quick, friendly email early in the year makes it much easier to reach out later if something comes up.

If your child has an IEP or receives extra support at school, September is the time to confirm that their plan has carried over and that their new teachers know about it. A short conversation now can prevent weeks of frustration.

Watch for Early Warning Signs

Some challenges show up quickly. Pay attention if your child:

Avoids talking about a particular subject. Silence about math class often means something is not clicking.

Takes much longer on homework than before. This can signal a gap from last year that is making new material harder.

Seems more tired or frustrated than usual. Adjusting to a new grade is normal, but ongoing stress is worth looking into.

None of these mean something is seriously wrong. They are just signals worth following up on.

Look Back at Last Year's Report Card

Last June's report card is one of the most useful tools you have right now. The comments often name specific skills your child was still developing, like fluency with multiplication facts or organizing ideas in writing.

Those gaps do not go away on their own. New units build on old ones, so a weak spot in Grade 4 fractions becomes a bigger problem in Grade 5. Addressing it in September is far easier than waiting until it shows up on a test.

Do Not Wait for the Report Card

In Ontario, elementary progress reports usually come home in November, and high school midterm reports arrive around the same time. If you wait until then to find out something is wrong, your child may already be two months behind.
