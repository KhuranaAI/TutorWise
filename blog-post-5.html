EQAO and the OSSLT: How to Help Your Child Prepare Without the Stress

May 15, 2026 TutorWise

Every spring, Ontario students in Grades 3 and 6 write the EQAO assessments in reading, writing and math. Grade 9 students write the EQAO math assessment near the end of their math course, and Grade 10 students take the Ontario Secondary School Literacy Test (OSSLT). For many families, this time of year brings a lot of questions and a fair amount of worry.

The good news is that these assessments are not something your child needs to cram for. With the right approach, you can help them walk in feeling prepared and confident.

What These Assessments Actually Measure

EQAO assessments are based on the Ontario curriculum. They check whether students can apply the reading, writing and math skills they have been building all year, not whether they memorized a list of facts the night before.

In Grades 3 and 6, students read short passages and answer questions, write responses that show they can organize their ideas, and solve math problems involving number sense, patterns, measurement and data. The Grade 9 math assessment covers the de-streamed Grade 9 math course. The OSSLT checks reading and writing skills that students need across every subject.

The OSSLT carries extra weight because passing it is one way to meet the literacy requirement for the Ontario Secondary School Diploma. Students who are not successful can try again or take the Ontario Secondary School Literacy Course, so one result does not define their future.

Why Cramming Does Not Work Here

Because these assessments look at skills built over time, last-minute drilling rarely makes a real difference. What does make a difference is comfort with the format and confidence in the basics.

A student who reads regularly, explains their thinking in full sentences and has strong math fundamentals is already in good shape. The goal in the weeks before the assessment is to keep those skills warm and remove any surprises about what the test looks like.

Practical Ways to Prepare at Home

Look at sample questions together. EQAO posts sample tests and practice materials on its website. Working through a few questions with your child takes away the fear of the unknown.

Practise explaining answers. Many EQAO math questions ask students to show their work or explain their reasoning. When your child solves a problem, ask them to tell you how they got the answer. If they can explain it out loud, they can usually write it down.

Read and talk about it. Ten to fifteen minutes of reading each day, followed by a quick chat about the main idea or a character's choices, builds exactly the kind of thinking the reading questions look for.

Write short responses. Give your child a simple prompt, like describing their favourite place or arguing for a later bedtime. Encourage a clear opening, a few supporting details and a closing sentence.

Supporting Grade 9 and Grade 10 Students

Older students often feel more pressure because they know the results go on their record or count toward graduation. For Grade 9 math, reviewing key topics like linear relations, proportional reasoning and algebraic expressions is more useful than redoing every worksheet from the year.

For the OSSLT, students benefit from practising the kinds of writing the test asks for, such as a short news report and a series of paragraphs that support an opinion. Knowing what each task expects can make a big difference to their confidence on the day.

Managing Test Anxiety the Week Of

Some children feel nervous no matter how well prepared they are. A few simple habits help:

Keep routines normal. Regular bedtimes and a good breakfast matter more than an extra hour of review.

Watch your language. Instead of "This is a big test," try "This is a chance to show what you have learned this year."

Remind them it is one snapshot. Teachers see your child's work every day. One assessment is a small part of a much bigger picture.

Keeping It in Perspective

EQAO and the OSSLT are useful tools for schools and families, but they are not the full measure of your child's ability. If the results show a gap, that is valuable information, and it is something that can be worked on.
