Getting Ready for the Fall Progress Report and Parent-Teacher Interviews

October 15, 2026 TutorWise

By mid-October, the school year is well underway. Units are moving forward, the first tests have come back and teachers are starting to put together their first formal reports. Over the next few weeks, most Ontario families will receive a progress report and have a chance to meet with their child's teacher.

Going in prepared helps you get the most out of both.

What the Elementary Progress Report Tells You

For students in Grades 1 to 8, the fall progress report is a little different from the report cards that come later in the year. Instead of letter grades or percentages, it shows whether your child is progressing very well, progressing well or progressing with difficulty in each subject.

It also rates learning skills such as responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative and self-regulation, using E for excellent, G for good, S for satisfactory and N for needs improvement.

A rating of "progressing with difficulty" is not a failing grade. It is an early flag, and it is exactly what this report is designed to catch while there is still plenty of time to respond.

What High School Families Should Expect

Secondary students usually receive a midterm report partway through the semester with a percentage mark in each course, along with learning skills ratings and teacher comments. Because high school courses move quickly, a midterm mark is a strong indicator of where the final mark is headed.

Before the report arrives, ask your teen how each class is going and check marks on their online classroom. There should be no big surprises on report day.

Questions Worth Asking at the Interview

Parent-teacher interviews are often short, so it helps to arrive with a few questions ready:

What is my child doing well? Strengths are useful to know and good to share with your child.

Where are they struggling, specifically? "Math" is too broad. Ask whether the issue is a particular skill like fractions, problem-solving or explaining their thinking.

How do they behave when they get stuck? Do they ask for help, give up or keep trying? This tells you a lot.

What can we do at home? Teachers often have simple suggestions that make a real difference.

If your child has an IEP, ask how their accommodations are working in class and whether any changes are needed.

Talk With Your Child Before and After

Before the interview, ask your child how they think school is going and whether there is anything they want you to mention. Kids often have a good sense of where they are struggling, even if they do not volunteer it.

Afterwards, share the positives first. Then talk about one or two areas to work on together. Keep the tone calm and focused on solutions. The goal is to help them feel supported, not judged.

Turn Feedback Into a Plan

The real value of the progress report comes from what you do next. Pick one or two specific goals based on what you heard, decide how you will work on them and check in again in a few weeks.

That might mean a regular reading routine, extra practice with math facts or checking assignments together each Sunday. For some students, it might mean bringing in outside help to rebuild a skill that has been shaky for a while.

The next formal report card comes home in the winter, which leaves a few months to make real progress.
