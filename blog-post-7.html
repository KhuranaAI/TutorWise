Avoiding the Summer Slide: A Simple Plan for Brampton Families

July 15, 2026 TutorWise

By mid-July, school feels like a distant memory for most kids. That is exactly how summer should feel. But two months away from regular learning can also mean that skills your child worked hard on during the year start to fade. Teachers often call this the summer slide.

The good news is that preventing it does not require worksheets every morning. A few small habits can keep your child's skills sharp while still leaving plenty of room for summer fun.

What the Summer Slide Looks Like

Research has shown for years that many students lose ground over the summer, especially in math. Teachers in September often spend the first few weeks reviewing material from the previous grade before they can move forward.

At home, it might look like a child who was confident with multiplication in June suddenly counting on their fingers again, or a strong reader who now finds chapter books tiring. These skills have not disappeared. They just need to be used.

Reading Is the Easiest Win

If you do only one thing this summer, make it reading. Twenty minutes a day is enough to keep reading skills strong and build vocabulary.

Let your child choose what they read. Comics, graphic novels, sports magazines and books about their favourite video games all count. The Brampton Library runs a summer reading program that gives kids goals and small rewards, which can be a great motivator.

For younger children, reading aloud together still matters. For older students, talking about what they read at dinner keeps comprehension skills active.

Keep Math Facts Warm

Math skills slip faster than reading skills, so a little regular practice goes a long way. Fifteen minutes, three or four times a week, is plenty.

Use everyday moments. Have your child calculate the total at the grocery store, figure out how long a road trip will take or double a recipe.

Play games. Card games, board games and dice games build number sense without feeling like homework.

Review the tricky topics. Look at your child's June report card. If fractions or multiplication facts were a struggle, those are the best places to focus.

A Light Weekly Routine

Structure helps, but it should be gentle. One simple approach is to set aside a short learning block a few mornings a week, before screens and activities take over. Keep it short, keep it consistent and keep it positive.

Writing can fit in too. A summer journal, postcards to family or a short review of a movie they watched all give your child a chance to practise organizing their ideas on paper.

Try to plan the routine around your family's real summer. If you are travelling for two weeks, that is fine. Pick it back up when you get home, and use the last two weeks of August to ease back into a school-year rhythm with earlier bedtimes and a bit more reading.

When Summer Tutoring Makes Sense

For some students, summer is the best time to get extra help. There is no new material coming in, no homework competing for time and less pressure overall. That makes it ideal for filling gaps from the past year.

Summer tutoring is especially useful for students who finished the year below grade level in a subject, students moving into Grade 9 or a new school, and students who want a head start on a challenging course in the fall.

Leave Room for Summer

The goal is balance. Kids need rest, outdoor time and the freedom to be bored sometimes. A little bit of learning each week is enough to keep their skills from slipping without turning summer into a second school year.
